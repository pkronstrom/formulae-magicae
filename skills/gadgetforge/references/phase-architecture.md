# Architecture, components and feasibility

**Minimum inputs.** Product brief, concept and delivery intent. Carry forward what the user agreed about interaction, display, power behavior and critical placement. Use those decisions to filter parts and compare rough packaging. If a candidate would change an agreed product choice, take the concrete tradeoff back to the user with an updated sketch. Get preliminary supplier/process limits before committing to a board type or hard-to-place component.

**Capture `architecture`.** Output roles `architecture`, `parts` and `visual`. Show a block diagram and provisional packaging. Record exact part/package when selected, order codes, dated stock and placement observations, mechanical geometry and approved alternatives. Assign power, sensing, controls, compute, interconnect and enclosure responsibilities only as needed. The `compatibility` check compares voltage, current, timing, pins, shared bus addresses, total rail budgets and occupied space. The `scope` check captures applicability, decision authority, and unknowns that could change the selected option. Clarify major product or architecture changes with the user before accepting them.

**Start from parts the user has or wants.** Record whether the requirement fixes an exact part, an interface or type, or a preference. For on-hand parts, record quantity, markings, revision if known, and a photo or measurement where identification or fit depends on it. Keep Amazon, AliExpress or similar listing links as sourcing references. Record the selected listing variant and any seller or order details needed to identify it. Verify pinout and dimensions from the exact unit or reliable documentation. An unclear identity can inform a provisional layout with stated assumptions. Derive production footprints only from adequate evidence. Check whether the selected assembler can accept user-supplied parts and who installs them. If a fixed part conflicts with fit, function or delivery, show the tradeoff and obtain the user's agreement before changing it.

Use bounded research by consequential part rather than every BOM line. Each dossier needs its part identity, datasheet revision, condition and units, limitations, source location and affected work. Review important evidence yourself. Borrow a relevant [recipe](../assets/recipes/) and pin an instance after verifying applicability.

**Capture `feasibility`.** Use current architecture and its riskiest assumptions. Output roles `findings` and `visual`, such as a simulation plot, section or measured prototype photo. The `claims` check pairs each conclusion with its model, calculation or measurement and conditions. Analog simulation records source/load, supply, control ranges and model identity. An outdoor sensor projection keeps sleep, transmit and retry assumptions separate from measurements. Use ngspice/KiCad or another verified simulator when it can answer the question. Leave unsupported claims explicitly untested. A physical spike is optional.

**If the design uses a microcontroller, check suitability for the workload.** Translate the agreed behavior into a short resource budget: required peripherals and usable pins, response deadlines, peak RAM, flash or storage, and power in the modes that matter. Include concurrent work and any boot, update or recovery overhead that affects the budget. Compare the budget against the exact device/module, configuration, and relevant constraints from its recipe or a sourced dossier. Record assumptions and remaining margin. Datasheet limits and conservative estimates can settle clear cases. A tight or uncertain requirement may need a small build, benchmark or peripheral spike before committing to PCB design. Record which workload and configuration were exercised. Carry unresolved claims into the obligations and firmware handoff with a deadline. Apply the same check to a project with a user-supplied controller. Mark it inapplicable when the design has no MCU.
